Hopping Across Australia

How kangaroos and wallabies differ across Australia's states, change over time, and connect with population monitoring, wildlife management and rainfall.

01 — Where Do They Live?

Kangaroos and wallabies are found across Australia, but different species show very different geographic patterns. We begin with individual observation locations, then step back to compare how each species is distributed across states and territories.

Where are kangaroos and wallabies recorded?

Atlas of Living Australia observation records, 2021–2025. Circle size represents the number of observations in each 0.25° grid cell.

Key takeaway

Observation records are most concentrated in eastern and south-eastern Australia. Western Grey Kangaroo records are more visible in the south-west, while Red-necked Wallaby stands out in Tasmania.

Atlas of Living Australia observation records show where animals were reported. They should not be interpreted as population estimates.

Six species, six different state-level footprints

Each map shows how a species' observation records are distributed across Australian states and territories from 2021–2025. Darker states account for a larger share of that species' observation records.

Eastern species concentrate in NSW

New South Wales accounts for a particularly large share of Eastern Grey Kangaroo, Swamp Wallaby and Red-necked Wallaby observation records.

South Australia tells a different story

Red Kangaroo, Western Grey Kangaroo and Common Wallaroo records are much more strongly represented across southern Australia, especially South Australia.

One national map hides regional identities

Separating the six species reveals state-level patterns that are much harder to see when all observation records are shown together.

From geography to composition

Different species occupy very different state-level recording profiles. The next section turns the question around: instead of asking where each species is recorded, which species make up the profile of each state?

03 — From Observations to Population

Atlas of Living Australia records tell us where people reported wildlife, but observation activity is not the same as animal abundance. Government monitoring provides a different perspective: population estimates derived from formal survey programs.

What do official population surveys show?

Government estimates of monitored macropod populations within commercial harvest areas in five Australian states, 2020–2023.

The previous chart showed changes in observation records. Official monitoring tells a different story: some surveyed populations rise while others fall, and those patterns do not necessarily mirror public wildlife records.

Queensland rises strongly

Queensland fell in 2021 before climbing to around 17.7 million by 2023.

NSW peaks before falling

New South Wales increased to almost 11.9 million in 2022 before falling to about 9.6 million in 2023.

Western Australia moves downward

Western Australia's monitored population declined from about 2.4 million in 2020 to around 1.7 million in 2023.

Observation records and population estimates tell different stories

A rise in wildlife reporting does not necessarily mean that the monitored population increased. Government survey data provides the population evidence used for management decisions.

DCCEEW population estimates cover surveyed commercial harvest areas, not complete state or national populations.

04 — From Population to Management

Population estimates are used to establish sustainable commercial harvest quotas. But a quota is a permitted maximum, not a prediction of how many animals will actually be harvested. Comparing quota and reported harvest reveals how much of that allowance is used.

How much of each sustainable quota was harvested?

Reported harvest compared with sustainable quota across five states in the Australian Government's 2024 macropod statistics. A full bar represents 100% of the sustainable quota.

The monitored population determines how much commercial harvest can be permitted, but the permitted quota is not necessarily reached. The gap between quota and harvest varies substantially between states.

Hover over a bar to compare the sustainable quota with the reported harvest count.

Victoria stands out

Victoria recorded harvest equal to 72.7% of its sustainable quota, by far the highest proportion among the five states.

Most quotas remain largely unused

NSW and WA used roughly one-third of their sustainable quotas, while Queensland and South Australia remained closer to one-fifth.

The five-state total is only 26.8%

Across all five states, reported harvest amounted to just over one-quarter of the combined sustainable quota.

A quota is a ceiling, not a harvest target

None of the five states reached its full sustainable quota. But one year alone cannot tell us whether these differences are temporary or part of a longer management pattern.

Sustainable quota percentages exclude special quotas.

How consistently do states use their sustainable quotas?

Harvest as a percentage of sustainable quota across five Australian states from 2021 to 2024. Darker cells indicate that a larger share of the permitted sustainable quota was harvested.

The 2024 comparison showed Victoria far ahead of the other states. Looking across four years reveals whether that difference is a one-year exception or a persistent management pattern.

Hover over a cell to see the quota utilisation for that state and year.

Victoria remains the highest

Victoria had the highest quota utilisation in every year shown, reaching 72.7% in 2024.

South Australia stays low

South Australia's reported utilisation remained below 25% throughout the four-year period.

Other states move differently

NSW and Western Australia increased in 2024, while Queensland declined from its 2022 level.

Quota utilisation has a strong state-level pattern

The contrast between states persists across multiple years rather than appearing only in 2024. Yet management is only one part of the story. Wildlife populations also exist within changing environmental conditions.

Values show reported harvest as a percentage of sustainable quota. Special quota figures are not included in the reported percentage.

05 — Rainfall and Population

Population management happens within a changing environment. Rainfall can influence vegetation, water availability and habitat conditions. Comparing rainfall and population on the same indexed scale helps reveal whether they tend to move together through time.

Do population changes move with rainfall?

Rainfall and monitored population are indexed to 2020 = 100, allowing their relative changes to be compared on the same scale from 2020 to 2023.

A value of 100 represents each state's 2020 level. Values above 100 indicate an increase from 2020, while values below 100 indicate a decrease. If the two lines move together, rainfall and population changed in the same direction.

The dashed line represents rainfall and the solid line represents monitored population. Hover over a point to see the original value.

NSW moves together most clearly

Rainfall and monitored population both increased through 2022 before falling below their 2020 levels in 2023.

Queensland shows divergence

Rainfall increased in 2021 while monitored population fell substantially. By 2023, population rose above its 2020 level even though rainfall had declined from 2022.

There is no single national pattern

South Australia, Victoria and Western Australia also show years where rainfall and population move differently, suggesting that rainfall alone cannot explain population change.

Rainfall provides context, not a complete explanation

Some states show periods where rainfall and monitored population move together, while others show clear divergence. The comparison reveals association rather than causation: habitat, reproduction, mortality, survey coverage and management may also contribute to population change.

Both series are indexed separately within each state using 2020 = 100. Population figures are DCCEEW estimates for surveyed commercial harvest areas. Rainfall is Bureau of Meteorology annual area-average rainfall. NSW rainfall uses the NSW/ACT series.
